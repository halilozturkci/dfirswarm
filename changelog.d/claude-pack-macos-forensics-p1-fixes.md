### Fixed: knowledgec_query stops at its own clock, and fsevents_parse reads a file of many gzip members in linear time (macos-forensics)

Two reads of the pack's four tools could outrun their time limit and be killed with nothing to show.

- **`knowledgec_query` has a deadline.** It had none: a database of 2.3 million rows (697 MB, inside the 2 GiB staging default) was still being read when a 125-second cap killed it, with no JSON on standard output and a 2.6 GB partial `out_file`. `max_seconds` (default 80, at most 90, below the manifest's 120) is now checked by a progress handler inside the statement, so even the sort of a table with millions of rows is stopped, and at every row. When it passes the answer is `partial`, the entries are a prefix of the result in start order (whole in the file the answer names, and `out_file` is kept), and `resume` gives `rows_read`, `stopped_after_z_pk`, `last_start_raw`, `after_start_raw` and `after_z_pk` (run again with the last two and the read goes on at the next row, none twice and none skipped) and `resume_since`, a microsecond before the last start read, the older way, which reads the rows at the last start again. Measured on that database with the cap at 125 seconds: before, killed at 125 s, 122 s of CPU, 0 bytes of output; after, rc 0 at 82 s (81 s of CPU, 130 MB resident), one JSON answer, `partial`, 230,912 entries and a 308 MB file of whole rows, `resume` given (how many rows 80 seconds read depends on the load of the machine, which was high). The cost per row is higher now, since every string passes the boundary below.
- **`fsevents_parse` finds the gzip member of a page by bisection.** `member_at()` rebuilt the list of every member's offset for every page: 100,000 members (a 2.8 MB file) took 46 seconds and about 270,000 passed the manifest's 300; the offsets are kept in a list of their own now. After: 100,000 members take 1 second and 270,000 (7.5 MB) 3. A test reads the CPU of 20,000 and of 80,000 members and fails when four times the members costs more than nine times the CPU.
- **`fsevents_parse` has a deadline and a restricted `contains`.** `max_seconds` (default 200, at most 250, below the manifest's 300): when it passes the file in hand is closed as partial at the page it was reading (`problems` names the offset), the files after it are `not_attempted` in `per_file`, `time_limit` says how to go on, and what was decoded is in the file. `contains` ran an unbounded regular expression over a path chosen by whoever wrote the volume: `(a|aa)+$` over a path of 40 characters took 37 seconds (14 s at 38), and the growth is x2.6 per two characters. It now runs a restricted subset (at most 256 characters; no back-reference, conditional, lookaround, atomic group or possessive repeat; no repeat of 9 or more inside any repeat, no repeat at all and no alternation inside a repeat of 9 or more; at most two repeats of 9 or more, at most eight repeats of any size), refused as JSON with the reason otherwise (fourteen catastrophic patterns are tested, each refused in milliseconds), and each search is given 0.5 second: a path that runs past it is counted in `filters.contains_timeouts` and the answer is `partial`. The subset cannot rule out the polynomial cases (`[a/]*[a/]*b` over 200,000 characters), which the timer bounds.

### Security: macos-forensics tools no longer answer a probe of a withheld name, print a cut token as hexadecimal, or run past their clock (macos-forensics)

The new boundary was applied wrongly in two ways in the three tools that print evidence, and four of its claims were not true. Every item below is a test that fails on the tools before it (`tests/pack-macos-forensics-guards.test.ts`, 22 tests: 20 fail on the previous tools; the other two state that `plist_read` has neither defect and that random bytes are not taken for a token).

- **`knowledgec_query`'s `stream` was an oracle on a withheld name.** It was an SQL `LIKE` over the stored stream name while the answer printed the name withheld and the row count in full: `ghp_` and the next characters of the hidden name, followed by `%`, gave `entry_count` 1 for a true prefix and 0 for a false one, at most 62 calls per character. The pattern is now matched against the name as the answer prints it (case-insensitive, `%` and `_` wildcards); the rows of a withheld stream that it did not match are counted in `stream_blind_rows` and the answer is `partial`; the count is the same whatever the hidden characters are (a test builds two databases that differ only in the hidden token and compares every pattern). `<withheld%` selects the withheld streams.
- **The place of a withheld name gave its byte rank away.** The stream list (`ORDER BY 2 DESC, 1`), the table list, `fsevents_parse`'s `per_file` (the directory's sorted listing, read in that order) and, in `unified_log`, the key names of a record with no entry's fields, the unreadable directories of the census and the members in both trees were ordered by the raw name: the placeholder stood where the hidden name sorted (40 of 40 runs for tokens that sort first, in the middle and last). They are ordered by the text printed, streams of one count likewise, the withheld ones in an order that is new for every run (the block's `print_order`, which none of the three tools called). `plist_read` has neither defect: it prints key and file names whole and withholds by the key's name.
- **A token cut by NULs or invisible characters was printed whole as hexadecimal.** The block reads hexadecimal as runs of printable bytes, and a token with a NUL or U+200B every 15 characters or fewer shows no run that looks like a secret: 1,000 of 1,000 plants were printed in a BLOB's head, 990 to 996 of 1,000 in the head of a longer BLOB, 1,000 of 1,000 as a `ZUUID` BLOB, and 1,000 of 1,000 in `fsevents_parse`'s `path_raw_hex` (the same tokens in a text field: 0 of 1,000). Now 20.1 seconds (wall 30.1 s, the answer's sealing of 95 rows) and 3.2 seconds (wall 6.4 s); the record or row in hand is judged first. Staging a database with a sidecar has the clock too: past it nothing is read, the answer is `partial` and the copy is removed.

A SIGKILL of the tool itself leaves the staged copy in `out_dir`, which the manifest says.

Every use of `predicate` is warned about in the answer, and a job run as secret output refuses one that names `eventMessage`, `composedMessage` or `formatString` (`predicate_names_message_text`).
- **Shapes the boundary did not claim, measured and said.** A FileVault personal recovery key (six groups of four capitals and digits) was printed in 29 to 32% of plants in a value, a stream and a BLOB's head, 30 to 47% in `unified_log`, 95% as a path component and 35% as a file name; a rule of the guard block withholds it (0 of 100 as a value, a stream, a BLOB's head and a log message, 0 of 200 as a path component). The id of a user's temporary folder under `/var/folders` (30 characters that the block takes for a token; all of 1,000 sampled paths under `/private/var/folders` of one Mac had text withheld, 77 were replaced whole) is shown as `tmp-id-` and 12 digits of its sha256, the same for the same id, when the block would withhold it (176 of the 1,000 have text withheld now, 33 are replaced whole). Printed, and said in the three manifests, the skills and the README: a BitLocker recovery key, a Wi-Fi-password-shaped token, short base62 tokens, text written as spaced characters, HTML entities, hexadecimal with spaces or percent escapes.

Repeats of 2 to 8 inside one another may now multiply to 16 at most; the pattern is refused in milliseconds with the reason, `(\d{1,3}\.){3}\d{1,3}` and `(?:[0-9a-f]{2}:){5}[0-9a-f]{2}` still run. The claims that were false are corrected in this file, the three manifests and four skills: the cut-token shapes, 11 of 1,000, 'the tool's own clock' for a clock read every 256 rows, and 'thirteen catastrophic patterns' (fourteen are tested).
